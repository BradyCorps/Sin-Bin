# SINBIN design north star

**Status:** Mechanical discovery validated by the creator; broader validation pending  
**Control:** `control/live-shift-lab-v1` at `11bc77dadc19bf319b96632464e4e29935eb6fd7`  
**Current experiment:** `experiment/live-shift-lab-2a`

## The game in one sentence

> Rewire your line in real time to keep the scoring play alive.

This is the defining SINBIN action. The player does not only construct a roster and watch it resolve. They operate the roster as a living machine, replacing pieces while possession, fatigue, opponent pressure, and the clock continue to move.

A representative sequence is:

`Retriever exits → Grinder enters → possession survives → Playmaker reacts → Sniper remains open → Chain continues`

One substitution should be capable of transforming several connected effects.

## Why this is the invention

The live line change combines pleasures that were previously separated:

- **Engine construction:** the roster determines which combinations are possible.
- **Engine operation:** the player actively rewires that machine during play.
- **Real-time manipulation:** thinking consumes time; the state does not wait politely.
- **Voluntary danger:** a successful but tired combination can be left out for more value.
- **Hockey-specific logic:** players cycle out of the offensive zone while the team tries to maintain pressure.
- **Immediate regret:** when a play dies, the player should understand the change that might have saved it.

The emotional sentence is:

> I can keep this ridiculous play alive if I make the change correctly.

The mechanic should remain understandable to a non-hockey player. Hockey supplies intuitive imagery and structure, not required specialist knowledge.

## Evidence from the control

The first Live Shift Lab was deliberately austere: rectangles, labels, one-minute play, visible fatigue, live substitutions, disruptions, safe dumps, and intentional penalties.

The creator reported that it was short, quick, fun with rectangles, and made their hands sweat. That is enough to pass the first internal design gate:

> Live rewiring is inherently fun for the intended creator-player.

It is not yet evidence that:

- The tension survives many sessions.
- Strategy, rather than only countdown stress, produces the excitement.
- Different rosters create different ways of thinking.
- Other hockey players understand and replay it.
- Non-hockey players understand and replay it.
- The interaction can support progression, collection, and a full run.

Those questions must be tested rather than assumed.

## Protected ingredients

Future experiments should preserve these unless one is the declared test variable:

1. **The machine remains live.** The clock and developing play continue while the player decides.
2. **The player changes pieces, not a risk slider.** The decision is which skater to replace, with whom, and when.
3. **Effects are causal and visible.** The player can follow why Pressure grew or why possession broke.
4. **Fatigue creates attachment and danger.** The best current combination is often difficult to remove.
5. **There is a safe release valve.** Dump and change sacrifices accumulated value to reset safely.
6. **The SIN BIN changes time.** Intentional penalties create an immediate benefit, a weakened interval, and a meaningful return.
7. **Runs are short and retryable.** Failure should produce an immediate alternative plan.
8. **Replay is comparable.** The same roster and opponent sequence allow a genuine “I should have done that” retry.

## Design pillars

### Operate, do not spectate

The most interesting decisions occur while the engine is firing. Roster construction matters because it changes live possibilities, not because it creates a larger automatic total.

### Create futures, do not match colours

A disruption must allow several defensible responses. The player chooses the future shape of the play rather than identifying one designated counter.

For example, pressure from a forecheck might be answered by:

- A safe bridge that preserves possession but lowers the multiplier.
- A fatigued rover who keeps the multiplier but risks collapse.
- A grinder who absorbs contact and changes the next sequence.
- An intentional penalty that overcharges now and creates a later disadvantage.

### Let specialists complete the machine

A weaker bridge skater should sometimes be more valuable than a higher-rated star. Roster value comes from possibilities, timing, and fit—not a single power number.

### Make risk state-dependent

Leaving a tired player active and taking an intentional penalty should sometimes be inspired and sometimes be a mistake. If either action becomes correct whenever available, it has collapsed into routine input.

### Make failure legible

Regret only causes replay when the player understands the alternative. A broken chain should state the failed link, relevant fatigue or disruption, and what category of effect could have preserved it without prescribing one exact answer.

### Prefer interaction over content volume

Short effects that combine in surprising ways are more valuable than a large catalogue of isolated traits. The mechanic must deepen before the content pool widens.

## Live Shift Lab 2A

### Question

> Does live rewiring remain compelling when the player operates two genuinely different machines, and does the mechanic reward planning rather than only fast counter-matching?

### Hypotheses

- **H1 — Roster identity:** Relay and Overload rosters produce visibly different substitution patterns and priorities.
- **H2 — Planning:** Players begin anticipating changes two or more decisions ahead.
- **H3 — Specialist value:** The correct live change is sometimes a lower-rated bridge rather than the strongest available skater.
- **H4 — Multiple responses:** The same disruption supports several viable responses with different downstream costs.
- **H5 — Cadence:** The mechanic remains interesting at a slower decision interval. If it only works at the five-second control pace, SINBIN may be primarily reflex-driven; that is a classification result, not automatically a failure.
- **H6 — Diagnostic replay:** A failed play creates a specific alternative plan and encourages replay under identical conditions.

### Pass signals

- The player replays without being prompted.
- The two rosters produce different change patterns, not merely different scores.
- The player knowingly retains a tired skater to preserve a valuable chain.
- One substitution triggers several understandable consequences.
- The player can explain why the play died.
- A weaker specialist becomes essential in at least some states.
- Players describe plans that span multiple upcoming changes.
- The slower condition retains meaningful decisions even if its emotional tone differs.

### Kill or redesign signals

- The best policy is always to replace the most-fatigued skater.
- Each disruption has one obvious counter.
- Bench order and roster composition barely affect decisions.
- Roster differences are mostly numeric strength.
- The player watches totals rather than the trigger chain.
- The SIN BIN is used automatically whenever ready.
- Most enjoyment still occurs before the live game.
- A generic “continue combo” button could replace substitutions without losing anything.
- Added explanation makes the game slower without making outcomes clearer.

## Progression, only after 2A passes

The likely macro-loop is:

> Construct the roster → order the bench → operate the live cycle → survive the consequences → acquire the missing component → run it again.

The next test after 2A should be a three-game miniature campaign with one face-up recruit choice between games. It should test whether the player naturally thinks:

> That play died because I lacked a bridge skater; I need this recruit.

Do not add currency, a shop, permanent upgrades, or win-gated recovery to that first campaign test.

Mechanical skaters should ultimately be unlocked through play, challenges, and discoveries. Cosmetic variants can provide shiny-card appeal without withholding mechanics or relying on paid randomized acquisition.

## Deferred presentation direction

The established future world is a slightly seedy, glamorous analog sports empire informed by real 1970s winter-sport fashion and Canadian athletic graphic design:

- Fitted athletic silhouettes, racing stripes, high collars, and front zippers.
- Saturated colour blocking, knitwear, nylon, leather, satin, goggles, and sculptural equipment.
- Smoky arena tunnels, amber wood panelling, chrome, coloured plexiglass, and aging civic arenas.
- Magazine covers, trading cards, instant-film photography, airbrushed posters, and analog broadcast graphics.
- Celebrity, rivalry, endorsements, scandal, and theatrical attention around the penalty box.

This replaces the earlier clean Atomic Age direction. It should not enter the rectangle lab until the mechanics earn expansion. When presentation arrives, it must amplify instantaneous role recognition and causal feedback rather than obscure the playfield.

## Decision rule

When considering any feature, ask:

1. Does it make live rewiring clearer, deeper, or more expressive?
2. Does it create a new decision rather than more administration?
3. Can its value be felt with rectangles and text?
4. Can we identify which hypothesis it tests?
5. Can we remove it cleanly if the test fails?

If the answer is no, defer it.
